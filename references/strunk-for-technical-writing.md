# Strunk's rules for technical writing

William Strunk Jr. taught English at Cornell University and privately printed *The Elements of Style* in 1918 as a short guide for his students. The full text is in `elements-of-style-1918.md`, and the rule numbers below match it. This file says which parts apply to technical writing today, how each applies, and which parts to skip.

## Rules that apply

### Chapter III: Principles of composition

These ten rules are the core of the book and apply directly.

- **Rule 9. Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic.** Each paragraph should cover one topic, and a new paragraph tells the reader that a new step has begun. Strunk also says single sentences should not stand as paragraphs, except for transitions. A reply made of one-line paragraphs, or of a heading over every two sentences, breaks this rule.

- **Rule 10. Begin each paragraph with a topic sentence, and end it in keeping with the beginning.** The first sentence tells the reader what the paragraph is for. The sentences after it explain, support, or develop that point, and the last sentence stresses the point or states a consequence. Do not end on a side issue or a minor detail. In technical writing, this means the first sentence of a paragraph should state its claim, not set a mood.

- **Rule 11. Use the active voice.** Active sentences are more direct and shorter. Strunk also warns against using a noun for the whole action and leaving the verb nothing to do: "A survey of this region was made in 1900" becomes "This region was surveyed in 1900." The passive is fine when the thing acted on is the topic of the paragraph.

- **Rule 12. Put statements in positive form.** Make definite assertions and avoid timid, non-committal language. "He did not think that studying Latin was much use" becomes "He thought the study of Latin useless." Use "not" to deny or to contrast, never to dodge a claim.

- **Rule 13. Omit needless words.** A sentence should have no unneeded words and a paragraph no unneeded sentences. Strunk is explicit that this does not mean every sentence must be short or that detail must go; it means every word should do work. Phrases such as "the fact that," "the question as to whether," and "he is a man who" are his standard examples. For AI writing, the needless words are usually filler, hedges, and framing, never the context the reader needs.

- **Rule 14. Avoid a string of loose sentences.** A paragraph made entirely of "X happened, and Y happened" sentences becomes monotonous. Vary the structure so that it shows the real relations between the ideas.

- **Rule 15. Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form.** Parallel ideas deserve parallel grammar, so the reader can see that they are parallel. Strunk adds that writers who vary their wording just for variety look undecided. For many similar items, he suggests grouping them or putting them in a table.

- **Rule 16. Keep related words together.** Do not separate the subject from its verb with a long phrase that could go at the start. Put modifiers next to the words they modify ("He found only two mistakes," not "He only found two mistakes").

- **Rule 17. In summaries, keep to one tense.** Do not drift between past and present. When summarizing someone else's work, say once that it is a summary instead of repeating "the authors state" in every sentence. In a review or related-work section, Strunk's advice is to build an argument supported by evidence, not a paper-by-paper summary with occasional comment.

- **Rule 18. Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.** The end of a sentence is where the new and important information belongs. The same holds for the last sentence of a paragraph and the last paragraph of a section.

### Chapter II: Rules of usage

- **Rules 1 to 7** are still standard English usage. They cover possessives, the serial comma, commas around parenthetical expressions, commas before "and" or "but" joining two clauses, comma splices, sentence fragments, and dangling participles. Rule 6, "do not break sentences in two," is the classic statement of Rule 2 of this skill: fragments such as "Coming home from Liverpool to New York." should be joined to the sentence they belong to.

### Chapter V: Words and expressions commonly misused

Most of this chapter still holds, and its opening note is worth remembering: the fix for a vague word is usually not a different word but a definite statement. Entries that matter most for technical writing:

- **Fact:** use it only for things that can be checked directly, not for judgments.
- **Factor** and **feature:** usually add nothing; say the thing directly.
- **Case, character, nature, system:** often redundant ("acts of a hostile character" is "hostile acts").
- **Due to:** not a substitute for "because of" in adverbial phrases.
- **Less / fewer:** "less" for quantity, "fewer" for number.
- **One of the most:** a worn-out opening for a paragraph or an essay.
- **Along these lines, literally, very, so (as an intensifier):** use sparingly or not at all.
- **While:** use it for "during the time that," not as a vague substitute for "and," "but," or "although."
- **Respectively:** usually removable. In formal technical writing it can be necessary; check that the sentence would not be clearer without it.

## Parts to skip

- **Rule 8 (dividing words at line ends)** is about typesetting by hand and does not apply to text written on a computer.
- **Chapter IV (matters of form)** covers handwritten manuscripts, ruled paper, and literary citation formats. Follow your venue's style guide instead.
- **The "They" entry in Chapter V** tells writers to use "he" for "everybody," "anyone," and similar words. Modern style guides, including those of the American Psychological Association and *The Chicago Manual of Style*, accept singular "they," and so does this skill.
- **The "Would" entry**, which asks for "should" in first-person conditionals, is outdated American usage.
- **Spellings** such as "to-day" and "to-morrow" (end of Chapter VI) are outdated. The rest of Chapter VI, a list of commonly misspelled words, is still useful.
- **Examples** in the book are drawn from literature and history. The principles transfer to technical writing even when the examples do not.
